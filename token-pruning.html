Systematic Benchmarking of Token-Pruned Large Language Models for Korean-Centric Applications

Hoyeol Kim, Hyeonwoo Kim

Vocabulary pruning as an efficiency lever for Korean-centric LLM deployment

Motivation

  • Multilingual LLMs carry general-purpose vocabulary and weights for dozens of languages, but most Korean-centric deployments only ever need a fraction of that capacity.
  • Token pruning — removing tokens and their associated training data for irrelevant languages — promises to cut memory and compute, but it's unclear whether it costs anything on the tasks that actually matter for Korean applications.
  • This work systematically benchmarks token pruning across nine model families to find out.

Method

  • Models: Qwen3 (0.6B–14B), Gemma-3 (270M–12B), Llama-3.1/3.2, Tri (1.8B–7B), Ministral-8B, Phi-4-mini, Aya-23/Expanse (8B), and Seed-X-PPO-7B.
  • Configurations: each model is compared Original (full multilingual vocabulary) against two pruned variants — EnKo (English + Korean only) and EnKoZh (+ Chinese) — with tokenizers and positional encodings reprocessed to keep alignment intact.
  • Evaluation areas: General Aptitude (KMMLU), Culture & Language (HAERAE, CLIcK), Instruction-Following (LogicKor, KoMTBench), and Machine Translation (WMT 24++ Korean–English), plus an inference-latency measurement on Seed-X-PPO-7B.

  • General aptitude and cultural understanding are preserved — pruned models track the original within the third decimal place on KMMLU, HAERAE, and CLIcK.
  • Machine translation quality is consistently maintained or improved: third-language tokens appear to act as interference during Korean–English alignment, so removing them can help.
  • Instruction-following results are more varied, but several models (Qwen3-14B, Gemma-3-12b-it) score higher after pruning on LogicKor — plausibly a regularization effect from a smaller, more focused vocabulary.
  • No consistent winner between EK and EKC: tasks in this study don't lean on Chinese-vocabulary knowledge, so the smaller EK vocabulary is the more efficient default.

Takeaway: for Korean-centric deployments, vocabulary pruning is close to a free lunch — it shrinks the model and modestly speeds up inference while core Korean task performance holds or improves.
